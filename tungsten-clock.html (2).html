Executive Summary

Operation Epic Fury, the 39-day U.S.–Israeli air and missile campaign against Iran from February 28 to April 7, 2026, did not simply expose a temporary munitions shortage. It exposed the material contradiction at the center of contemporary American power: the persistence of a doctrine of global primacy alongside an industrial base increasingly unable to reproduce the material conditions that doctrine requires.

This paper argues that Epic Fury should be understood not as an isolated episode of wartime depletion but as a diagnostic event. It revealed a structural contradiction between hegemonic aspiration and material reproduction. Tungsten is treated not as the sole bottleneck in American war production but as a concentrated index of this contradiction — an input through which the gap between strategic doctrine and industrial capacity becomes visible, measurable, and dated.

  • The United States expended more than 1,000 Tomahawks, roughly half its Patriot interceptor stockpile, and up to 290 THAAD interceptors in 39 days of combat — against a prewar baseline already reduced by the 2025 Twelve-Day War.
  • Replenishment timelines run to late 2030 for Tomahawks, mid-2029 for Patriot, and late 2029 for THAAD — years of industrial capacity consumed at a tempo the doctrine treats as episodic and manageable.
  • China's dominance over tungsten processing, combined with its February 2025 export controls and January 2026 whitelist, converted a supply-chain dependence into a strategic timing instrument. APT prices rose from ~$350/mtu to above $3,100/mtu by April 2026.
  • The Time Compression Trap — the 35–90× asymmetry between China's 60–90 day disruption timeline and the West's 7–15 year mitigation timeline — is not a projection. It is the operating assumption under which current planning now appears to proceed.
  • The Winchester Trap is not the moment inventories run to zero. It is the condition of a force that has consumed years of replenishment capacity against an adversary whose doctrine — Iran's "decentralized mosaic defense" — is built to make the conflict last years.
  • The institutions best positioned to address this contradiction are, by the dynamics documented in Section VI, structurally least able to name it: organized denial is not only cognitive — it is structural and interested, sustained by actors with material stakes in the procurement-gap framing.

Abstract

Operation Epic Fury exposed a structural contradiction between a doctrine of global primacy and industrial and logistical conditions that no longer reproduce themselves at the pace the doctrine requires. Tungsten is the most legible index of that contradiction, linking the consumption of force to the material base required to regenerate it. The paper introduces the Time Compression Trap — the stacked asymmetry between China's disruption timeline and the West's mitigation timeline across consumptive, productive, and ecosystem material layers — and the Winchester Trap, the condition in which a force exhausts years of replenishment capacity against an adversary whose doctrine is built for attrition. It argues these limits cannot be resolved through procurement alone, but require a reckoning with the political economy that produced them and the organized denial that sustains it.

I. The Clock That Was Already Running

On February 28, 2026, the United States and Israel launched Operation Epic Fury, a sustained air and missile campaign against Iran that opened with the assassination of Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei and strikes on nuclear infrastructure, air defense networks, and command-and-control facilities, and triggered Iranian retaliation against seven countries within 48 hours and an extended closure of the Strait of Hormuz. The campaign's high-intensity phase lasted 39 days, ending with a ceasefire brokered in Islamabad on April 7 that has remained contested and incomplete in the weeks since. This paper does not attempt to assess the war's outcome as a whole — its regional escalation, its decapitation of Iran's leadership, or its global energy-market effects are each subjects requiring their own analysis. Its scope is narrower and, for that reason, more tractable: the relationship between a 39-day campaign's consumption of U.S. precision-munitions inventory and the multi-year timelines required to replace what was consumed. By the official Western record, the campaign's military phase registered as a success: facilities were declared degraded, air defenses attrited, escalation contained. But Iran absorbed the assault without regime collapse or strategic capitulation, while imposing significant material costs on the coalition prosecuting the war — so the campaign has to be read through the asymmetry between operational damage and strategic endurance, not through strike effects alone.

What the press releases did not say — and what no press release is designed to say — is that the United States emerged from the campaign with more than 1,000 Tomahawk cruise missiles expended, Patriot interceptors reduced by between 1,060 and 1,430 rounds, and THAAD batteries that had consumed an estimated 190 to 290 interceptors in theater.1 Replenishment timelines run to late 2030 or early 2031 for Tomahawks, mid-2029 for Patriot, and late 2029 for THAAD. The Navy has a gap. The gap is measured in years. The financial dimension compounds the material one: CSIS estimated the campaign's opening phase cost roughly $891 million per day, more than 94% of it unbudgeted, with total direct military expenditure reaching approximately $35 billion by June 2026 — a scale that, on CSIS's own 52-month manufacturing lead-time finding, money alone cannot compress into an operationally relevant timeline.66

One clarification of scope: the United States is not incapable of fighting or winning a large-scale war. The depletion data above describes a 39-day campaign against a single regional power, and even at current attrition rates the United States retains the capacity to prosecute such a campaign, defend its territory, and impose devastating costs on any adversary. The claim is narrower and, for that reason, harder to dismiss: that the United States cannot sustain the doctrine of simultaneous-theater primacy — readiness for major confrontation with China and Russia while also conducting episodic campaigns against Iran and its aligned networks — at the production rates and reserve levels its current industrial base supports. A single intense campaign against a single adversary revealed the gap. The doctrine assumes that gap does not exist across three or four simultaneous fronts. That is the claim under examination.

Stated at the level of theory, the paper's contribution is this: the United States' doctrine of global primacy presupposes an industrial and organizational base that its own political economy no longer reliably reproduces, and Epic Fury is the event that made that presupposition empirically testable rather than merely asserted. This is a different question from the one most policy literature on munitions and critical minerals asks. CSIS and similar studies — including the report this paper relies on most heavily — ask how to rebuild inventories and accelerate production; their implicit frame treats the shortfall as a procurement problem with a procurement solution. This paper asks a prior question: why does the doctrine continue to presume inventories, surge capacity, and supply-chain sovereignty that the underlying economy no longer consistently provides, and what does it mean that the institutions responsible for the doctrine have not, on the evidence reviewed in Section VI, been able to pose that question to themselves? The contradiction of primacy is the widening gap between the geopolitical scope a hegemonic power claims and the material infrastructure required to support it. The hegemonic-decline literature has long identified this gap under headings like "imperial overstretch," recently reframed as "hegemonic overreach" to emphasize that it is sustained by policy choice as much as by relative economic decline. That reframing matters because it locates part of the contradiction in mission as well as material capacity: a hegemon's sense of its own necessary role can compound a material constraint rather than merely respond to it, predating and deepening the gap this paper traces. Recent work on the unraveling of U.S.-led hegemony into a more contested, multiplex arrangement tracks the same gap at the level of institutional order.48 Tungsten is not the theory. It is the index — the input through which the contradiction becomes visible, measurable, and dated. The political-economy genealogy behind this paper's argument runs through Arrighi's account of financial expansion as the signature of hegemonic decline — the observation that capital's withdrawal from productive activity into financial accumulation is not incidental to American overextension but constitutive of it — and through Bluestone and Harrison's earlier documentation of the same process at the level of deindustrialization, applied here to the defense sector specifically.68 The sections that follow trace that index through the campaign's munitions arithmetic (Sections I and III), the temporal architecture that makes the arithmetic structural rather than incidental (Section II), the export-control regime that converts the structural condition into an instrument of strategic timing (Section IV), the deeper political-economic and geopolitical reordering the contradiction belongs to (Section V), the institutional mechanism by which the contradiction is misrecognized (Section VI), and what would be required — and what would not be sufficient — to address it (Section VII).

Four frameworks do the work of this argument, each brought in where the evidence requires it rather than all at once: Ian Clark's primacy-hegemony distinction, which names what kind of problem this is (Section V); Giovanni Arrighi's account of hegemonic financialization, which explains the historical mechanism producing it (Section V); Farrell and Newman's weaponized interdependence, which explains how the resulting dependencies become instruments of timing (Section IV); and Janis, Allison, Oreskes, and Conway on organizational misrecognition, which explains why the institutions responsible for naming this structure cannot do so (Section VI). Together they answer four questions — what kind of problem, why it emerged, how it became weaponized, and why it persists unaddressed — that converge on a single empirical event. Tungsten is the index. The four frameworks are the theory.

II. The Time Compression Trap

The structural precondition for the Winchester Trap was established long before February 28, 2026. The Time Compression Trap is the asymmetry between China's disruption timeline and the West's mitigation timeline. China can impose meaningful disruption on U.S. munitions production within 60–90 days. The minerals are embedded in weapons systems that cannot be rapidly reformulated. Western governments, by contrast, face minimum 7–15 year timelines to build alternative supply chains. Permitting, environmental review, construction, and processing capacity must all be created from scratch in jurisdictions where the relevant industrial infrastructure does not exist at scale. The numbers are unambiguous.

The economic architecture underlying these ratios compounds the timeline problem. Western alternatives face not only a temporal barrier but a structural cost penalty: facilities serving only defense customers at low volumes cannot approach the unit economics of Chinese producers operating at civilian-market scale across electric vehicles, wind turbines, and consumer electronics. The result is a permanent 5–10× cost disadvantage for non-Chinese producers, making economic sustainability dependent on continuous government subsidy.

MP Materials — the Pentagon's flagship domestic rare-earth investment, backed by a $400 million Defense Production Act commitment — reported losses of $23 million against $61 million in revenue in its first quarter of 2025, not from mismanagement but from this structural arithmetic.5 Even if Western governments solved the timeline challenge, the economic trap would persist. A further and underreported dimension: more than 60% of Western tungsten scrap currently flows to Chinese recycling facilities. Even battlefield material recovery, under present supply-chain conditions, risks feeding adversary processing capacity.

There is a further refinement to the Time Compression Trap that the 7-to-15-year figure understates, because it implicitly treats the problem as one of capital and permitting — both of which respond, however slowly, to money and political will. The more accurate framing, increasingly argued by industry analysts tracking the rare-earth buildout, is that extraction is not the binding constraint at all. Rare earth deposits are geologically common; what China has built over three decades is not access to ore but the separation chemistry, metallurgical know-how, and equipment base required to turn ore into usable metals, alloys, and magnets — a capability one industry voice summarized as "you can permit a mine faster than you can replicate that institutional knowledge."32 China's share of rare-earth mining is commonly cited at around 55–60%, but its share of magnet manufacturing is closer to 95%, and its share of the equipment used to manufacture magnets approaches 100%.32 This is a different and harder problem than the tungsten case primarily illustrates. Tungsten's Western shortfall is substantially a capacity and stockpile problem — mines and refining lines that were not built or were allowed to close. The rare-earth processing shortfall is a tacit-knowledge problem: a body of accumulated institutional experience in separation chemistry that does not transfer through capital alone and has no predictable acquisition timeline, because "buy more of it faster" is not an available strategy for experience. The scale of exposure this creates is considerable: a 2024 analysis by the defense-data firm Govini found that more than 80,000 distinct parts across approximately 1,900 weapons systems depend on minerals — including tungsten, antimony, gallium, germanium, and tellurium — now subject to Chinese export controls, with Navy systems particularly exposed.33 Per-platform rare-earth content is illustrative of the same exposure at the level of finished systems: estimates place the F-35's rare-earth content at roughly 400–420 kg, an Arleigh Burke-class destroyer's at roughly 2,400–2,600 kg, and a Virginia-class submarine's at roughly 4,200–4,600 kg, with some variance across sourcing methodologies.34 This refinement matters for Section VII's Tier Two recommendations below. "Industrial policy realism" — accepting a permanent cost premium for strategic production — is a sound response to a capacity problem. It is not, by itself, a sufficient response to a tacit-knowledge problem, because no amount of subsidized capacity converts institutional experience that does not yet exist into experience that does. The honest implication is that even the most aggressive Tier Two posture narrows the Time Compression Trap's denominator more slowly, and on a less certain timeline, than the 7-to-15-year figure — itself already daunting — suggests.

Before documenting the gallium case, the concept needs a structural upgrade. The tungsten ratio above remains accurate on its own terms, but the gallium and silicon cases reveal that the trap is not a single ratio — it is a stacked temporal structure, where different materials bind different layers of war-making capacity at different timescales. The distinction matters analytically because it explains why solving the tungsten problem does not close the vulnerability: the layers are independent, their timelines are different, and China holds chokepoint instruments at each layer. The gallium case makes this structural argument with a second, independently documented index. Where tungsten is the critical input for kinetic energy penetrators and shaped-charge munitions, gallium is the critical input for gallium nitride (GaN) semiconductor chips — the beating heart of every modern active electronically scanned array (AESA) radar and electronic warfare system. China controls approximately 99% of the world's primary low-purity gallium production.51 The United States has not recovered primary domestic gallium since 1987; its net import reliance as a percentage of reported consumption has been 100% every year from 2021 through 2025.52 When China imposed an outright ban on gallium exports to the United States in December 2024, the U.S. National Defense Stockpile held zero gallium reserves.51 The Department of Energy's response was $6 million in R&D funding — against an import price already at $580 per kilogram before the ban, and against a Chinese production base of approximately 900,000 kilograms annually against the rest of the world's combined 9,000 kilograms.52 War on the Rocks assessed the gallium situation in April 2026 as the United States "repeating its silicon mistake" — the same pattern of offshoring strategically critical semiconductor manufacturing, retaining design capability while surrendering production, and discovering the consequence when geopolitical pressure converts trade dependence into a supply cutoff.53 Tungsten and gallium are not two separate problems. They are two expressions of the same political-economic choice, made across four decades, to treat industrial capacity as a cost center rather than a strategic asset — presenting their bill simultaneously, in the same conflict, against the same adversary.

III. The Burn Rate and the Winchester Trap

Operation-level data from Operation Epic Fury reveal not only a munitions shortage but a new form of strategic paralysis. The Winchester Trap — named for the old Western film idiom of a gunfighter declaring himself "Winchester" when he has fired his last round — is not, on its own terms, a claim that the United States ran out of munitions during the 39-day campaign. CSIS's own assessment is explicit that it did not: inventories were depleted but remained sufficient to continue the fight as it was actually being fought.6 The trap is what that finding implies once it is read alongside the adversary's actual doctrine rather than in isolation. A campaign that consumed one to four years of replenishment capacity in 39 days was not fighting the kind of war Iran's military doctrine is built to fight. It was fighting the brief, high-intensity opening phase of one — and even that phase, on CSIS's own numbers, exhausted the inventory cushion that a longer campaign would require.

In the first weeks of Epic Fury, U.S. surface combatants and submarines fired Tomahawk land-attack missiles at Iranian air defense sites, command nodes, and declared nuclear infrastructure. The Washington Post reported more than 850 TLAMs fired in the campaign's first month; the Wall Street Journal later reported an updated figure of more than 1,000 — a number CSIS notes could account for nearly all Tomahawks available in theater, given that ships cannot reload at sea and must return to port to rearm.39 Against a prewar inventory CSIS estimates at roughly 3,100, this represents a depletion of approximately 27 to 32 percent in 39 days. That baseline itself understates the cumulative pressure on the inventory: the same systems had already been drawn down eight months earlier in the 2025 "Twelve-Day War," when roughly 80 SM-3 interceptors and more than 150 THAAD interceptors were expended against Iranian ballistic missiles — depletion that CSIS's prewar figures for Epic Fury already reflect as a reduced starting point, not a separate event layered on top.40 Two depletion events inside one year, against the same adversary, are not the profile of an inventory built for the kind of war this adversary intends to fight. Raytheon has announced a production acceleration target toward approximately 1,000 Tomahawks annually as part of the FY2027 budget request, but CSIS's own methodology — congressional appropriation, then a 24-to-36-month manufacturing lead time, then 12 months to produce the full lot, "about 52 months in all" — places full replenishment at roughly four years from the FY2027 request, or approximately late 2030.41

The United States did not run out of ammunition in this war. It spent four years of replenishment capacity to fight the first month of a war its own adversary's doctrine was built to make last for years — while simultaneously accepting radar-less fighter jets as a "calculated risk" and holding zero strategic reserves of the gallium that the next-generation radar requires. That is the trap: not the moment the rack goes empty, but the arithmetic of a system depleting across three temporal layers simultaneously against a doctrine designed to make the conflict last until the depletion becomes decisive.

The THAAD picture is in some ways more consequential, and not only because of interceptor counts. Between 190 and 290 THAAD interceptors were expended in theater on top of the more than 150 already expended during the Twelve-Day War the previous year.7 CSIS identifies THAAD as the most critical of the seven munitions it tracks "due to their low inventory and lack of alternatives," with U.S. production running at 96 interceptors per year against a Lockheed Martin target of 400 announced under the FY2027 framework agreement — itself years from full effect.42 Beyond interceptors, the war produced a combat loss with no comparable replacement timeline at all: an AN/TPY-2 radar — the targeting system THAAD batteries depend on, of which only 13 exist in the entire U.S. inventory — was damaged or destroyed at a base in Jordan, a loss CSIS describes as creating a "capability gap" with no near-term fix.43 An Army official separately told reporters that the entire U.S. inventory of Precision Strike Missiles had been expended in the campaign, though other officials disputed the claim.44 For the Patriot PAC-3 system, the drawdown was comparably severe: 1,060 to 1,430 interceptors fired, representing roughly half the prewar stockpile, with replenishment estimated at roughly three years — and that is before accounting for allied demand, including more than 600 Patriot interceptors delivered to Ukraine over the course of the war and 17 other Patriot-equipped nations now competing for the same production lines.7 Japan, meanwhile, was informed its pending delivery of 400 Tomahawks — missiles central to its own buildup against China — would be delayed because of Epic Fury's consumption.45 A single 39-day campaign against one regional adversary measurably degraded an allied Pacific deterrent before a shot was fired in the theater that deterrent exists for.

And all of this concerns only the munitions side of the ledger. The sensor side tells a parallel story unfolding simultaneously. Starting in June 2025 — during the same period that the Twelve-Day War was drawing down U.S. interceptor stocks — the U.S. military began accepting deliveries of F-35 Joint Strike Fighters without their primary radar installed. The APG-85, the next-generation GaN-based AESA radar that Lot 17 aircraft were designed to carry, could not be installed on schedule because of delays rooted in gallium nitride supply-chain constraints and engineering problems that GaN's substantially higher power demands (approximately 82 kilowatts, against earlier configurations) created for the aircraft's bulkhead and cooling architecture.54 Northrop Grumman confirmed the delay; the F-35 Joint Program Office confirmed that Lot 17 aircraft — deliveries running through September 2026 — would be received without their advanced radar installed.55 Aircraft delivered without the APG-85 are not combat-coded and are restricted to training use until retrofitted, with a dual-compatible bulkhead solution not expected until Lot 20, deliveries beginning 2028. To maintain aircraft center of gravity in the absence of the radar, ballast plates are installed in place of the APG-85 — a detail reported in Korean-language defense media coverage of the JPO program and corroborated by the program office's own description of the aircraft's "metal plate" configuration — converting an administrative statement into a visible operational reality: the most advanced fighter in the U.S. inventory is being delivered with a weight plate where its primary sensor belongs.55 Representative Rob Wittman, a senior member of the House Armed Services Committee, stated the outcome plainly: the U.S. military will be left with "lots of aircraft out there, but not ones that are ready to go to the fight."54 The Pentagon's official characterization was that the APG-85 delay represented "a calculated risk the JPO needed to take" under a "highly concurrent development and production program." That language — "calculated risk," "concurrent" — is worth holding alongside the munitions data above. The United States entered Operation Epic Fury with interceptor inventories already reduced by the Twelve-Day War, accepted radar-less combat aircraft as a "calculated risk" in the months before and during the campaign, and emerged from the campaign with missile stocks requiring years to replenish and a sensor capability gap requiring years to retrofit. These are not independent failures. They are simultaneous expressions of the same structural condition: a production system whose architecture cannot keep pace with the consumption rate its own doctrine generates.

CSIS frames the resulting risk as prospective: depleted inventories will constrain "a future conflict... particularly against a peer competitor like China." That framing is correct but incomplete, because it treats the relevant future conflict as necessarily a different war, in a different theater, against a different adversary. Iran's strategic doctrine — publicly articulated by its own officials as "decentralized mosaic defense" — was built explicitly to survive a decapitating opening strike, preserve distributed command and missile capacity, and convert any conflict into a protracted contest of attrition designed to exhaust the adversary's stocks and raise the political cost of continuation over months or years.46 The sharp drop in Iranian missile and drone launches after the first week — 83 and 90 percent respectively, the decline CSIS cites as the reason U.S. inventories proved sufficient — is consistent with exactly this doctrine: a deliberate transition from a brief high-intensity phase to a sustained, lower-cost campaign of harassment that the doctrine was designed to make affordable for Iran and unaffordable for its adversaries to counter at the same tempo. In the weeks after the April 7 ceasefire, Iran intermittently closed and reopened the Strait of Hormuz, maintained a naval blockade posture alongside the U.S. Navy's own blockade of Iranian ports, and continued missile and drone harassment of Gulf states and U.S. positions — low-cost, sustainable pressure of exactly the kind mosaic defense doctrine is designed to apply indefinitely.47 The "future war" CSIS warns about is not necessarily a separate, later conflict. By its own logic, it may already be the second phase of this one — fought against a doctrine explicitly designed to make depletion the adversary's problem rather than its own, in a theater where the United States has already spent down the inventory CSIS says any such contest requires.

Anticipating the Objection: Surge, Substitution, and Allied Production

Three objections to the depletion argument recur in policy discussion, and each deserves a direct answer. The first is that wartime mobilization can surge production well beyond peacetime rates, so peacetime inventory ratios are not the relevant baseline. The second is that allied industrial contributions — South Korea, Japan, Europe — can offset American shortfalls. The third is that depletion of any given munition is expected in war and does not, by itself, indicate systemic incapacity.

Against the first: the Russia-Ukraine war has been a live, multi-year test of exactly this proposition for 155mm artillery shells. At the start of 2024, U.S. monthly production stood at approximately 30,000 rounds against a stated wartime-mobilization goal of 100,000 by the end of 2025.18 By May 2025, output had reached only approximately 36,000 — a fraction of the target.18 The EU's parallel ASAP program moved from roughly 300,000 shells annually toward an estimated 1.4 million by the end of 2024, still short of its own one-million-shell pledge and well below the roughly 4.2 million Russia reached annually by 2025 with North Korean supplementation.19 Surge production is not impossible; even under sustained political pressure and funding, it moves at a pace measured in low multiples over years, not the order-of-magnitude response the "surge capacity" objection typically implies. Applied to Tomahawk, THAAD, and Patriot — systems with longer lead times and far smaller production bases than 155mm shells — there is no basis in the comparable case for expecting a faster outcome.

The second objection is addressed by the same case from the other direction: South Korea's shell transfers to Ukraine were transfers of existing stockpile, not evidence of allied surge capacity sufficient to close a structural gap. A fuller version of this objection holds that the relevant unit of analysis is an allied industrial network rather than the U.S. base alone — and naval shipbuilding is the clearest current example. Facing what the U.S. government itself describes as decades of deterioration in commercial shipbuilding, the Navy has turned to South Korean shipbuilders at unprecedented scale: Hanwha Ocean has performed maintenance and overhaul work at its Geoje shipyard, and under the 2025 "Make American Shipbuilding Great Again" framework, South Korean firms have pledged up to $150 billion toward U.S. shipbuilding, with Hanwha committing $5 billion to convert its Philadelphia-based Philly Shipyard into a warship-construction facility.50 This is real integration of allied industrial capacity — and, on inspection, evidence for this paper's argument rather than against it, for two reasons. First, it is happening because no domestic alternative exists on any usable timeline; the Navy is substituting allied capacity for a domestic capability, not supplementing a healthy one. Second, shipbuilding capacity and munitions production capacity are not the same industrial base: Hanwha's investment addresses hull construction and does nothing for Tomahawk, THAAD, or Patriot lines, which remain dependent on a small number of specialized U.S. manufacturers operating under the consolidation dynamics described in Section V. The allied-network reframe describes how the United States now sustains its surface fleet. It does not describe a parallel mechanism for precision-munitions inventories — and the speed at which Washington has had to accept this dependence illustrates how acute the capacity gap has become, not how available a substitute exists for munitions specifically.

The third objection — that depletion is expected and not systemic — is the one this paper accepts most readily, with a qualification. Depletion in any single campaign is indeed expected. What is not ordinary is the combination documented in Sections I through III: a single regional campaign against one of several anticipated adversaries consumed inventory on timelines the doctrine assumes remain available for simultaneous fronts. The Ukraine-era data shows this is not a one-off — it is a structural characteristic of the relationship between Western industrial capacity and sustained high-intensity conflict of any kind.

IV. China's Export-Control Architecture

The tungsten story begins with a bureaucratic document. On February 4, 2025, China's Ministry of Commerce published MOFCOM Announcement No. 10 of 2025, imposing export controls on tungsten, tellurium, bismuth, molybdenum, and indium — all materials with direct military and advanced manufacturing applications. The announcement was widely noted in trade press and almost entirely absent from mainstream security analysis.

The price effect was immediate. Ammonium paratungstate — the intermediate product from which tungsten metal is refined — was trading at roughly $350/mtu on European spot markets in early 2025. By January 2026, the CIF Rotterdam price had reached $900–940/mtu, already at record levels. By the time Operation Epic Fury began on February 28, 2026, Fastmarkets was quoting $1,650–1,900/mtu — a near-500% rise in twelve months. By April 2026, as the war reached its ceasefire, APT broke above $3,000/mtu in Rotterdam.8 Mining.com reported the above-$3,000 threshold on April 29, explicitly linking the move to surging military demand.

The February 2025 tungsten controls did not occur in isolation, and the chronology of what followed matters for understanding the whitelist's place in the broader architecture. On September 29, 2025, the U.S. Bureau of Industry and Security implemented the "Affiliates Rule," extending Entity List, Military End-User List, and SDN-linked export restrictions to any foreign entity 50% or more owned, directly or indirectly, by a listed party — the most significant expansion of U.S. export-control reach in years.35 China's response, ten days later, was the largest single escalation of its export-control regime to date. On October 9, 2025, MOFCOM issued a package of announcements (Nos. 55–58, 61, and 62) that for the first time asserted Chinese extraterritorial jurisdiction: a 0.1% de minimis rule and a foreign-direct-product rule requiring a Chinese export license for any foreign-made good containing Chinese-origin rare-earth content above that threshold, a parallel 50% ownership rule mirroring the U.S. Affiliates Rule, case-by-case licensing for semiconductor and AI end-uses involving logic chips at 14nm and below or memory at 256 layers and above, and — critically — controls extending beyond the rare-earth materials themselves to the equipment and technical know-how used to mine, smelt, separate, and process them, alongside new controls on high-performance batteries above 300 Wh/kg, battery anode and cathode materials, and industrial diamonds and superhard materials.36 Roughly one month later, on November 7, 2025, both governments agreed to a mutual one-year suspension of this October package as part of a broader trade de-escalation, with the suspension running until November 10, 2026.37 The tungsten-specific architecture — the original February 2025 Announcement No. 10 controls and the December 2025/January 2026 whitelist discussed below — was not part of either the October escalation or the November suspension. While the headline rare-earth and semiconductor measures were escalated and then shelved within six weeks as bargaining chips in a broader negotiation, the tungsten whitelist proceeded on its own track, untouched by the truce. This is itself evidence for this paper's argument: China made the clock legible and usable as leverage precisely because the tungsten architecture was not folded into the high-profile, reversible measures that both sides have shown a willingness to trade away under negotiating pressure. The whitelist did not create the dependence. It converted an already-existing dependence into an instrument with predictable strategic timing.

The architecture did not stop at price controls. In December 2025 — effective January 2026 — China's Ministry of Commerce designated just 15 state-authorized companies permitted to export tungsten for the 2026–2027 period.9 This whitelist model, confirmed by Asian Metal and reported by Investing.com and Reuters, represents a qualitative escalation: it shifts the control mechanism from licensing friction to supply-chain gatekeeping. The 15 approved exporters include China Tungsten & Hightech Materials Co. (a core China Minmetals subsidiary) and Xiamen Tungsten Co. — both vertically integrated from mining through processing, both state-directed. The effect is to route all legal tungsten exports through entities that answer directly to Beijing's strategic priorities. In the vocabulary Farrell and Newman developed for "weaponized interdependence," this is a textbook chokepoint node: a network structure in which one jurisdiction's regulatory authority over a small number of central nodes gives it disproportionate power over the flows that pass through them, independent of its share of the underlying physical resource.12 The whitelist does not increase China's share of global tungsten reserves. It increases Beijing's capacity to monitor, delay, and redirect the flows that already pass through Chinese processing — which is precisely the mechanism Farrell and Newman describe for the SWIFT payment network and the semiconductor supply chain, applied here to a critical mineral. China's own response to the September 2025 Affiliates Rule, described above, mirrored this logic exactly: a 0.1% de minimis and foreign-direct-product regime is the SWIFT-sanctions architecture turned around and pointed back at its designer, with rare earths in place of dollar clearing. The system is operational, not merely declaratory. By June 2026, documented enforcement cases confirmed the architecture's active posture: two Japanese nationals were detained in Dalian in May 2026 on allegations of rare-earth-related smuggling, one of the first known instances of foreign nationals detained in connection with a PRC export control violation involving strategic minerals; MOFCOM published Announcement No. 26 of 2026 (effective July 1, 2026) formalizing a public reporting mechanism for strategic mineral violations; and on June 22, 2026, China added ten U.S. entities to its export control list — including rare-earth miner MP Materials and magnet maker USA Rare Earth, the Pentagon's own flagship rare-earth investments — prohibiting any party worldwide from transferring Chinese-origin dual-use goods to them.65 Customs enforcement of the rare-earth dual-use controls has produced documented penalty cases: in one May 2026 case, a Chinese manufacturer was fined approximately RMB 910,000 — roughly 4.5 times the cargo value — after customs testing found 1.2% dysprosium in a shipment declared as ordinary NdFeB magnet, reclassifying it as a controlled dual-use item exported without a license.65 The fine-at-4.5×-cargo-value structure, combined with the detention of foreign nationals and the public reporting mechanism, constitutes an enforcement architecture calibrated to deter circumvention through misclassification and third-party brokers — the two principal routes through which sanctions-evasion networks have historically operated.

The volume data confirm the mechanism's effectiveness. According to China's General Administration of Customs, Chinese tungsten APT exports fell nearly 70%, from 782 tonnes in 2024 to 243 tonnes across the first eleven months of 2025 — a collapse achieved entirely through licensing friction before the whitelist system was even fully operative.10

To make the "clock" concrete rather than metaphorical: a standard 120mm kinetic energy penetrator round uses a tungsten heavy alloy core of approximately 4.0 to 4.6 kilograms, at 90–93% tungsten content by weight, with the remainder nickel, iron, and cobalt binder.20 Shaped-charge warhead liners — the component that determines a precision munition's penetration depth against hardened targets — have historically used tungsten at up to 35% of liner weight blended with copper, with newer high-performance liners pushing that fraction higher.20 Across the more than 1,000 Tomahawks, and the hundreds of JDAM, Hellfire, and JSOW munitions expended in a 39-day campaign, the cumulative tungsten draw is measured in tonnes, not kilograms — drawn from a global APT export pool that had already collapsed by roughly 70% before the campaign began. Substitution exists at the margins: depleted uranium remains an alternative for kinetic penetrators in the systems still cleared to use it, and some shaped-charge designs can shift toward higher copper fractions at a measurable cost in penetration performance. But no substitute matches tungsten's combination of density, hardness, and thermal stability at comparable cost, which is precisely why Chinese dominance of supply functions as leverage rather than as one input among interchangeable others.

Will Parry-Jones of Wolfram Advisory, presenting at the MMTA Vancouver conference in April 2026, characterized the current dynamic as a structural "scarcity trap" driven by Western underinvestment in primary tungsten production over the preceding decade.11 That framing is correct as far as it goes but insufficient: the scarcity is not the product of market neglect alone. It is the product of deliberate architectural choices by a state with dominant positional power in a chokepoint commodity. Parry-Jones offered a second observation to Reuters in January 2026, less remarked upon than the scarcity-trap framing but more analytically precise: "China has been proactively building its manufacturing capacities — it is now seen as the factory of the world… if as an end-use customer you can't physically buy your widget from your preferred supplier, then you may have no choice but to buy the widget from China. This plays perfectly into the Chinese plan."58 That sentence, from a tungsten market specialist with no theoretical ax to grind, states China's developmental logic more clearly than most academic literature does. The export controls are not purely coercive. They are also supply-side industrial policy: by restricting refined tungsten outflows, Beijing channels feedstock toward Chinese downstream manufacturers — cutting-tool makers, EV component producers, semiconductor fabricators, precision engineering firms — that compete against the same Western defense and industrial contractors now scrambling for supply.

The clearest illustration of this export-control architecture in effect is not a munitions case at all. The tungsten hexafluoride (WF6) case shows the Time Compression Trap completing one full cycle in real time, outside the munitions domain entirely. Tungsten hexafluoride (WF6) is the only commercially viable precursor gas for depositing conductive tungsten thin films in advanced semiconductor manufacturing — essential in sub-7nm logic chips, HBM memory, and 3D NAND, with no mature substitute. Japan's Kanto Denka Kogyo and Central Glass together account for roughly 25% of global high-end WF6 supply. In April 2026, both formally notified Samsung, SK Hynix, and TSMC that WF6 production would permanently cease effective July 1, 2026. The cause: China's January 2026 export control announcement halted high-purity tungsten powder shipments to Japan beginning in February; GAC data reported by Kyodo News and TrendForce shows Chinese tungsten powder exports to Japan were zero for three consecutive months from February to April 2026. High-purity tungsten powder constitutes 60-70% of WF6 production cost, and Japan has virtually no domestic tungsten resources. Japanese producers sustained operations approximately five months by drawing down inventory, attempted to purify lower-grade powder through existing distillation columns, and found it chemically impossible: molybdenum contamination in lower-purity tungsten produces gaseous compounds with boiling points nearly identical to WF6, making separation unachievable. The combined annual capacity loss is approximately 2,200 tons, roughly 25% of global high-end WF6 supply, with an estimated hard supply shortfall of 2,000 tons in the second half of 2026.64

Three things follow from this case for the paper's argument. First, it extends the Time Compression Trap to a third material-layer consequence of tungsten controls operating simultaneously with the munitions case: the same February 2026 controls pushing APT above $3,000/mtu were simultaneously triggering a semiconductor specialty-chemicals rupture that standard policy analysis was not tracking. Second, it demonstrates the dual-function logic of Chinese critical-mineral statecraft with unusual clarity: the WF6 shock drives market share toward Chinese WF6 producers (CSSC Gas surged 765% year-to-date by June 2026), converting a coercive restriction on Japan into a competitive advantage for domestic Chinese manufacturers. Third, it illustrates the tacit-knowledge constraint in its most unambiguous form: Kanto Denka and Central Glass had proprietary distillation technology, decades of experience, and long-term customer relationships with TSMC, Samsung, and SK Hynix — and none of it was actionable once upstream feedstock was cut. The 18-24 month qualification cycle for new WF6 producers means the supply tightness is projected to persist through 2027, reproducing the Time Compression Trap's core asymmetry at yet another layer of the semiconductor materials stack.64

China's Tungsten Architecture: Coercion and Development as a Single Instrument

The standard Western framing of China's export controls treats them as a coercive instrument — a weapon applied to adversary supply chains. This framing captures one function of the controls and misses the other. The two-function account is both more accurate and more alarming, because it explains why the controls are durable and why standard supply-chain diversification cannot fully address them.

China is simultaneously the world's largest producer and the world's largest consumer of tungsten. Tungsten carbide accounts for approximately 65% of global tungsten consumption, and the dominant applications — cutting tools, mining equipment, wear-resistant components, precision manufacturing — are exactly the industrial capabilities China has been scaling under "Made in China 2025" and its successor frameworks.59 China produced approximately 12.4 million electric vehicles in 2024, over 70% of global output, each requiring tungsten carbide tooling for battery fabrication, tungsten electrodes for aluminium welding, and tungsten-based electrical contacts.60 Beijing's domestic mining quotas for 2025 were cut 6.5% from the prior year not to contract the economy but to tighten supply discipline and prioritize high-value downstream processing over raw export — a deliberate industrial-upgrading decision.58

This dual character — defensive against foreign military users, developmental for Chinese industrial upgrading — is what distinguishes Chinese critical-mineral statecraft from the Western sanctions it mirrors. When the United States imposed export controls on advanced semiconductors, it was attempting to deny China a capability it did not yet have. When China imposed export controls on tungsten, it was simultaneously denying that capability to others and reserving the feedstock for its own industrial sectors whose competitive advantage depends on having it. The controls do not require China to choose between restricting adversaries and developing its economy. They accomplish both simultaneously. Project Blue estimates that global defence demand, currently approximately 12% of the tungsten market, is expected to grow to roughly 15% by 2027–2028 as stockpile replenishment sustains demand — confirming that the military demand shock is real but layered on top of pre-existing civilian tightness that was already driving prices before Operation Epic Fury began.61

The civilian price impact of the controls deserves its own emphasis because it complicates the narrative of pure strategic calculation. In the year following China's February 2025 export controls, tungsten prices more than tripled — not only in Rotterdam and London, where Western defense contractors were scrambling for supply, but inside China itself, where domestic APT prices hit record highs of $1,125–$1,150 per metric ton unit in January 2026.58 Chinese automakers, electronics manufacturers, precision tool producers, and heavy industrial firms faced the same price shock as their Western counterparts. This is not evidence that the controls failed. It is evidence that Beijing calculated the domestic cost as a manageable tradeoff — one that Chinese firms with state backing can absorb more readily than Western firms operating on market terms — while the strategic positioning benefit accrued regardless. The controls impose a cost on Chinese civilian industry; they impose a structurally larger cost on Western adversary industry; and the asymmetry in absorption capacity is itself part of the instrument's logic. Chinese state-directed capital can cross-subsidize input costs across strategic sectors in a way that no Western market-based defense contractor can replicate. The export control regime is therefore also a competitive stress test: it probes whether Western industrial firms can sustain the cost at which Chinese industrial firms — backed indefinitely by state capital — can operate.

The policy implication that the standard "diversification" response underestimates is this: building mines in Vietnam, Kazakhstan, Portugal, or South Korea addresses the extraction bottleneck. It does not address the processing bottleneck, where Chinese dominance is more complete and the tacit-knowledge gap is wider. It does not address the downstream-manufacturing advantage that Chinese firms have accumulated across EVs, electronics, and precision manufacturing. And it does not address the structural asymmetry in state-capital backing. Diversification narrows one layer of the vulnerability. The developmental logic of Chinese critical-mineral statecraft is operating across all three layers of the stacked Time Compression Trap simultaneously.

V. The Hegemonic Contradiction: Deindustrialization, Defense Capitalism, and Strategic Overreach

This discussion intervenes in the literature on hegemonic order by separating two concepts that the post-Cold War primacy debate has systematically conflated: material primacy and legitimate hegemony. The distinction matters for the paper's argument because it is the gap between them — not the absence of power, but the erosion of the material conditions on which durable order depends — that Epic Fury exposed. In the IR literature, hegemony is not synonymous with primacy. Primacy denotes a form of material power: the concentration of capabilities in a single state sufficient to dominate outcomes in any particular domain. Hegemony denotes something more demanding: a socially recognized form of leadership, embedded in rules, institutions, and legitimating practices, that generates a form of social power distinguishable from material dominance alone. Ian Clark's formulation is the most precise: hegemony is "a legitimate practice" giving rise to "a form of social power" irreducible to material primacy, and the relevant question for contemporary IR is not simply whether the United States remains the most powerful state but whether primacy has been, or can be, converted into the kind of institutionalized, legitimate order that makes its reproduction self-sustaining rather than continuously contested.62 The hegemonic stability literature, from Kindleberger through Gilpin and into the contemporary Brooks-Wohlforth debate on the costs of unipolarity, has tended to treat material dominance as the independent variable from which order follows — asking whether leadership will prove so costly that decline is generated by the leadership itself.63 Against purely materialist realism, this paper argues that power concentration is necessary but not sufficient for sustained order: legitimacy, institutionalization, and the capacity to reproduce the material conditions of leadership are equally required. Against overly voluntarist leadership accounts — which treat legitimacy as available on demand if the hegemon commits correctly — this paper argues that legitimacy cannot be assumed or performed when the material reproduction of primacy is slowing, because other states read the replenishment gap as evidence that the underlying commitment is itself becoming conditional. What Epic Fury revealed is precisely this: a doctrine of primacy persisting as aspiration and strategic posture after the industrial conditions required to make it self-reproducing had become increasingly fragile, delayed, and politically managed rather than seamlessly sustained. The paper's theoretical contribution is to show how that gap becomes legible at the level of ammonium paratungstate prices, THAAD replenishment timelines, and radar-less fighter jets — and why the institutions positioned to name it cannot. The most important direct contestation this framing implies is with Ikenberry's liberal hegemony account, which argues that American order is durable precisely because it is embedded in rules-based institutions that provide other states with incentives to participate — making it more than material dominance and capable of outlasting any particular power configuration. Against Ikenberry, this paper argues that institutional embeddedness is not a substitute for material reproduction: when the productive base required to sustain the credibility of security commitments erodes, other states' incentives to participate in the institutional order recalibrate accordingly, and no amount of rules-based institutionalization restores the prior relationship between American capability and allied confidence once the replenishment gap becomes visible and documented.71

Operation Epic Fury revealed a contradiction the Washington policy world prefers to discuss only in fragments. The United States is not running into an unfortunate supply-chain problem at the margins of an otherwise healthy military order. It is colliding with the cumulative consequences of its own political-economic model. For four decades, American elites championed a regime of offshoring, financialization, labor arbitrage, lean inventories, and industrial disassembly, then treated the resulting dependence as efficiency.27 Arrighi's account of hegemonic financial expansion supplies the theoretical logic for why this looked like efficiency from the inside: each hegemonic cycle culminates in a shift of capital from productive activity into financial speculation, which Arrighi reads not as a late-stage indulgence but as the structural signature of a regime of accumulation approaching its limits. Moore's account of capitalism's dependence on continuously replenished "Cheap Natures" — cheap labor, food, energy, and raw materials — extends the same logic to the resource base itself: offshoring tungsten and rare-earth processing to China was not a miscalculation but the rational operation of a political economy built on cheap inputs, until those same inputs became a source of leverage for the state that now controls their processing.68 What now appears as strategic vulnerability is not an external surprise. It is the return of neoliberal deindustrialization in military form.

This is why the critical-minerals question must be framed politically rather than technically. The United States did not innocently "lose" secure access to tungsten, gallium, germanium, antimony, and rare-earth processing. It helped build a world economy in which dirty, low-margin, strategically indispensable production was pushed outward while domestic accumulation shifted toward finance, services, intellectual-property rents, and high-end systems integration. Washington now narrates the outcome as a procurement challenge, as if the problem were insufficient foresight or underinvestment in a few key sectors. The deeper reality is harsher: the same ruling bloc that demanded globalization's efficiencies now confronts globalization's strategic bill. This is, in substance, the overextension thesis that the realist retrenchment literature has been making for over a decade, applied here to its material rather than its purely strategic dimension: a grand strategy organized around commitments that exceed the resource base required to sustain them generates exactly the kind of crisis this paper documents, regardless of whether the excess commitments are measured in troops, dollars, or tonnes of tungsten.28 The paper's specific theoretical move, relative to that literature, is to relocate the overextension argument from troop deployments and alliance commitments — the terrain on which Mearsheimer, Walt, Posen, and Layne have made it — to the industrial and mineral substrate that the retrenchment debate has generally treated as a background condition rather than as the binding constraint. The constraint is not that commitments are strategically unwise; it is that the productive base required to honor those commitments is no longer being reliably reproduced by a political economy that has spent four decades optimizing for something else. This treats the contradiction as a path-dependent structural condition — a political economy with powerful reinforcing incentives against the restructuring it requires — rather than as a deterministic impossibility; Section VII's Tier Two recommendations presuppose that restructuring is possible, while the paper's argument is that the institutional and financial architecture makes it improbable on any timeline that matches the operational consumption rate the doctrine assumes.

The gallium and gallium nitride cases are instructive precisely because they reveal that the pattern predates the current geopolitical rupture and extends across semiconductor technology domains, not just extractive minerals. Silicon is not a scarce raw material — it is abundant in the earth's crust. The chokepoint is fabrication: the advanced-node manufacturing processes concentrated on a single island 100 miles from mainland China, whose loss would not constitute a materials shortage but a production-ecosystem collapse requiring a decade or more to reconstitute anywhere else. The United States pioneered silicon semiconductor manufacturing, then systematically offshored it — training Taiwanese engineers through technology-licensing agreements in the 1970s, watching Intel become the first U.S. semiconductor company to offshore production in 1972, and arriving by 2024 at a world in which Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company controls nearly 70% of the global foundry market while the U.S. retains design and intellectual-property dominance without the manufacturing ecosystem to make the designs tangible at scale.53 The gallium nitride case is this pattern at an earlier stage: U.S. researchers invented and developed GaN technology; GaN production capacity migrated to Asia and is now effectively dependent on Chinese primary gallium; and the consequence — radar-less F-35s, a next-generation electronic warfare capability delayed — is already visible in the same conflict this paper documents, not in a hypothetical future one. What the silicon case took thirty years to produce, the gallium case is reproducing in a decade, against an adversary whose control of the primary input is not contested. The pattern is not a series of discrete policy failures. It is a reproduction of the same structural tendency — prioritizing financial returns on intellectual capital over the maintenance of production capacity — applied to each successive generation of critical technology. Tungsten is not an anomaly in this series. It is the latest instance of a process that has been running for fifty years.

The contradiction sharpens further inside the defense sector itself. The U.S. defense-industrial base no longer functions primarily as a war-production system. It functions as a rent-bearing security market.29 Its dominant incentives are not wartime volume, replaceability, durability, and surge responsiveness. They are cost growth, subcontracting complexity, proprietary lock-in, guaranteed demand, and shareholder extraction. That is why the United States can spend staggering sums on defense and still struggle to replenish basic inventories on anything resembling wartime timelines. The problem is not merely that weapons are expensive. It is that the production system is optimized for profitable scarcity, not mass reproducibility under pressure.

The consolidation of the sector from 51 major prime contractors in 1993 to five dominant firms is often presented as rationalization.30 It is better understood as oligarchic narrowing — and its mechanism deserves more attention than the number alone conveys. Consolidation eliminated the competitive pressure that historically disciplined cost growth: when Lockheed acquires Martin Marietta, when Northrop acquires Grumman, when Raytheon absorbs Hughes and then merges with United Technologies, the government loses the ability to threaten a defaulting or underperforming contractor with replacement, because there is no replacement. Cost-plus contracting — in which the government pays allowable costs plus a guaranteed profit margin — then removes the remaining incentive to hold costs down, since higher costs produce higher absolute profits. The result is a sector in which the dominant firms maximize revenue through program complexity, schedule extension, and proprietary subcomponent lock-in, because those are the behaviors the contracting structure rewards, regardless of whether they produce weapons at the volume and speed the doctrine requires. Fewer firms, fewer production nodes, fewer substitute suppliers, and more captive demand do not create resilience. They create a rentier architecture with national-security branding. In such a system, the extraordinary unit cost of weapons is not an accidental inefficiency. It is one of the ways the system works. The result is a military machine that remains technologically formidable yet materially brittle: highly lethal, highly capitalized, and structurally unable to regenerate at the speed its doctrine requires. Epic Fury made that brittleness visible. The replenishment problem documented in Sections I through III is not a temporary lag to be fixed by managerial urgency — it is the predictable outcome of a military order detached from an industrial order capable of sustaining it, the schedule of an empire outrunning its factory floor.

The central contradiction is not between American power and Chinese power alone. It is between a doctrine of permanent global primacy and an economic base hollowed out by the elites who insist on preserving that doctrine — a strategy class that treats simultaneous confrontation with China, Russia, and Iran, Red Sea operations, and alliance reassurance across two continents as modular commitments addable without consequence. They cannot be: they draw from the same inventories, minerals, production lines, and overextended fiscal state. The United States remains fully capable of territorial defense and of inflicting catastrophic violence abroad. That is not the issue. The issue is that destructive power is no longer the same thing as sustainable hegemony — the problem is not that America is weak, but that it remains organized around imperial scale after the material basis for imperial scale has begun to fail. China's export-control architecture matters not because it introduces vulnerability into an otherwise coherent American system, but because it reveals how incoherent that system already is: Beijing inherited an opening created by decades of U.S.-led restructuring, while Washington tried to preserve hegemony from the standpoint of finance and military command while allowing the productive foundations of that hegemony to decay. The present crisis is the result.

Contours of the Emerging Order

Everything argued so far risks being read inside the frame this paper has been trying to dismantle: that the question is how the United States restores the conditions for primacy. That framing is itself part of the contradiction. The Cold War and post-Cold War orders were organized around security blocs, in which economic relationships were subordinate to alliance structure. China's tungsten architecture, Iran's endurance, and the Epic Fury depletion data are symptoms of a different, already-operative order — one in which geoeconomic relationships, not security alignment, increasingly determine how states position themselves.13

The states most consequential to this shift rarely appear in this paper's data, because they are not party to the Iran campaign — yet their behavior is what gives the tungsten clock its meaning. India, Brazil, the Gulf states, Turkey, Indonesia, and Vietnam are not choosing sides in a U.S.–China bloc competition. Gulf states including Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Qatar have deepened economic ties with China while retaining U.S. security relationships specifically to preserve strategic flexibility.14 Goh's "omni-enmeshment" and Kuik's typology of hedging give this behavior analytical structure rather than leaving it as undifferentiated non-alignment.31 These states are accumulating relationships with both powers — hedging currency exposure, diversifying suppliers, joining overlapping frameworks including China's CIPS network and the BRICS-linked mBridge platform — whose cumulative effect is to make exclusive alignment costly and unnecessary.15 The dollar's share of global foreign-exchange reserves has fallen from roughly 72% in 2001 to under 58% by late 2024.16 None of these states needs to declare for or against American primacy; the geoeconomic order they are building makes the declaration increasingly beside the point. This is a trajectory, not a settled outcome — the Gulf states retain U.S. security guarantees while deepening ties with China, which is hedging, not replacement.

India is not a digression from this hedging pattern; it is the sharpest test of it, because it is the state U.S. strategic planning most needs to behave as an aligned partner and is most systematically declining to do so. It is simultaneously the largest contributor to the Quad critical-minerals working group and China's largest growing bilateral trade partner, with India–China trade reaching roughly $118 billion in 2024 despite the unresolved Galwan Valley standoff. New Delhi has repeatedly declined to join U.S.-led export-control coordination on advanced chips and dual-use minerals, citing sovereign industrial policy. The Quad pledge and the China trade relationship are not contradictory in India's logic — they are the same strategy: binding the United States into a security relationship it values while binding China into an economic relationship it cannot afford to lose.73

China's tungsten posture belongs to this same shift rather than standing as an isolated coercive instrument. The whitelist, the civil-military fusion model, and the willingness to run strategic sectors at thin margins for decades are features of an industrial strategy built for a world where production capacity, not alliance loyalty, is the durable form of leverage.17 The United States, by contrast, remains organized around the assumption that financial centrality, technological edge, and alliance structure are themselves sufficient instruments of order. They are powerful instruments. They are not the same kind of instrument as the ability to make what the world needs at scale.

None of this means China occupies this position from comprehensive strength. Roughly 80% of China's crude oil imports — worth approximately $312 billion in 2024 — transit the Strait of Malacca, a passage 1.7 nautical miles wide at its narrowest point and entirely outside Chinese control.21 Beijing has named this the "Malacca Dilemma" since 2003 and has spent two decades on overland pipelines, Arctic shipping routes, and strategic reserves without materially closing the gap.21 China's tungsten leverage and America's capacity to interdict China's energy lifeline at a single chokepoint are mirror-image vulnerabilities. This is not a reason to discount the tungsten clock; it is a reason to read it as one instrument among several in a system of mutual, asymmetric leverage — a description of multiplexity rather than of bipolar confrontation.

This is where the diagnosis becomes most uncomfortable for the policy world it describes. If the emerging order is geoeconomic and multipolar rather than bipolar and security-organized, the contradiction identified in this paper is not, even in principle, fully correctable by reindustrialization, stockpiling, or allied coordination within the existing doctrine. This does not mean America's remaining advantages — a still-dominant reserve currency, an unmatched alliance network, a technological edge in select domains — are illusory. It means they are levers for a kind of order, one in which Washington's preferences set the terms others adjust to, that is itself receding; no quantity of financial centrality or technological edge restores the prior relationship between American preference and global outcome once production capacity has migrated elsewhere. The honest version of the question this paper raises is not "how does the United States close the gap between its industrial base and its global commitments." It is what role remains available to a state with America's advantages in an order where those advantages no longer translate automatically into the primacy the doctrine assumes. The paper does not resolve that question; it is raised here because the recommendations that follow are answerable only in light of it.

VI. Organized Denial: The Washington Policy Imaginary and Its Structural Limits

One of the most consequential failures of Western analysis in the months preceding and during Operation Epic Fury was the treatment of Iranian military capability degradation as a verifiable fact rather than as an intelligence inference resting on structurally limited evidence.

Iran dispersed its missile infrastructure across a national territory of approximately 1.64 million square kilometers and 32 provinces in the years following the 2020 assassination of Qassem Soleimani. Western satellite imagery can observe facility construction, vehicle movement, and missile launch signatures. It cannot reliably inventory dispersed, mobile, and hardened systems. The HUMINT posture inside Iran — following the purges of suspected CIA and Mossad networks documented in 2021–2023 — provides limited capacity for ground-truth on production rates, dispersal locations, or operational readiness.

The result was a systematic overestimation of Iranian military degradation that persisted throughout the campaign. Western press accounts, drawing on official briefings, consistently described Iranian missile forces as significantly attrited. The April 7 ceasefire, negotiated in Islamabad under Pakistani and Qatari mediation, was announced alongside official U.S. and Israeli claims of having "substantially degraded" Iranian capabilities. The claims were not verifiable. They may or may not be accurate. They should not be treated as the evidentiary foundation for the next set of strategic decisions.

This discussion builds on the organizational and institutional failure literatures by treating non-recognition not as a cognitive failure but as an institutional effect of strategic and budgetary incentives. Against cognitive explanations of denial — which treat the problem as one of ignorance or analytical error correctable through better information — this paper argues that the relevant problem is incentive alignment: the actors best positioned to name the structural contradiction have the strongest organizational reasons not to. Against purely rhetorical accounts of denial — which focus on the performative evasion of inconvenient facts — the more useful claim is that denial is endogenous to hegemonic maintenance itself: the more costly it becomes to sustain primacy materially, the more institutionally valuable the procurement-gap framing becomes, because that framing allows the system to acknowledge scarcity without conceding the deeper contradiction between global ambition and industrial capacity. The most important theoretical move is therefore not to show that Washington misread the evidence — the CSIS depletion data is published and widely read — but to show that the institutions responsible for acting on it are, for structural reasons the paper traces through Janis, Allison, and Jones and Silberzahn, the institutions least capable of drawing the conclusion the evidence supports.

This epistemic failure is therefore larger than a misreading of Iran. It is a failure of the Washington strategic imaginary itself. Official analysis can acknowledge stockpile depletion, delivery delays, and supply-chain dependence, but it remains structurally unable to state the deeper proposition to which those facts point: that the United States is confronting the accumulated consequences of deindustrialization, defense-sector financialization, and hegemonic overextension. What cannot be admitted in Washington is not merely that a particular campaign may have underperformed. It is that the material foundations of full-spectrum dominance have eroded to the point where operational success can coexist with strategic deterioration. The CSIS report names the "window of vulnerability" with precision and then frames it as a problem of insufficient procurement — correct as a description of symptoms, unable to name the system producing them, because naming it would require saying that the doctrine of simultaneous global primacy is no longer supported by the industrial economy it presupposes. That is why the failure is not only analytic but epistemic: it names a reality visible at the level of inventories, lead times, and mineral chokepoints, yet remains politically unspeakable within a policy culture organized around the presumption of enduring primacy.

This denial is not a malfunction of the system. It is how the system reproduces itself. CSIS produces depletion analyses that confirm the problem in granular empirical detail and then recommend larger contracts, faster procurement, and expanded appropriations through the same institutional channels whose structural incentives produced the gap — flowing to the same five prime contractors, through the same cost-plus contracting structures, that created the profitable scarcity the paper documents. This is not bureaucratic inertia. It is a productive contradiction: the contradiction of primacy is simultaneously a problem to be named and a resource to be extracted by the organizations naming it. The system does not break down in the face of this dynamic. It thrives on it — which is precisely why procurement reform, on its own terms, cannot resolve a contradiction it materially depends on sustaining.

Groupthink and the Policy Imaginary

"Organized denial" is not a metaphor improvised for this paper. It names a documented organizational pathology, and the documentation is instructive precisely because it includes a case in which the pathology was successfully avoided. Irving Janis's analysis of groupthink identified a specific cluster of antecedent conditions — high stress from a perceived external threat, insulation of the decision-making group from outside expertise, homogeneity of background and outlook among group members, and the absence of a procedure for systematic dissent — that together produce premature consensus and the suppression of unwelcome information.22 Each of these antecedents maps onto the contemporary national-security policy community with uncomfortable precision: the perceived China threat supplies the stress; the revolving door between the Pentagon, the intelligence community, and the small set of consultancies and think tanks that supply both personnel and analysis supplies the insulation and the homogeneity; and the absence of any institutional actor whose role is to ask whether the doctrine itself, rather than its execution, is the problem supplies the missing dissent procedure.

The instructive contrast is the Cuban Missile Crisis — not because the crisis itself resembles Epic Fury, but because the Kennedy administration's executive committee process (ExComm) was deliberately structured to defeat groupthink, and its structure succeeded.23 Participants were assigned to argue positions they did not hold; outsiders including George Ball were brought in specifically because they were not invested in prior decisions and could say, without career cost, that a proposed course of action was wrong. The result was a decision process that considered options — including a naval quarantine rather than an air strike — that the antecedent organizational dynamics would have suppressed. Graham Allison's subsequent analysis of the crisis through an "organizational process" lens supplies the second half of the theoretical apparatus this paper needs: much of what appears as strategic choice is in fact standard operating procedure executing without reconsideration, because reconsidering it would require the organization to admit that its own prior outputs — budget requests, procurement decisions, doctrine documents — were the proximate cause of the problem now being diagnosed.24 The Pentagon cannot easily conclude that deindustrialization is structural, because that conclusion implicates the budget priorities the Pentagon itself advanced for forty years. The present policy culture has no ExComm. It has the institutional conditions Janis identified, and none of the correctives Kennedy's administration built. More recent work on intelligence failure reaches a similar conclusion from a different direction: Jones and Silberzahn's study of CIA strategic surprises — including, notably, the Cuban Missile Crisis itself as a near-miss alongside cases of outright failure — argues that what looks retrospectively like an "intelligence failure" is more often an organizational-culture failure, in which warnings that contradict institutional identity are systematically discounted regardless of their evidentiary quality, and the analysts who issue them (their "Cassandras") are marginalized not because they were wrong but because being right would have been organizationally inconvenient.49 Bar-Joseph and McDermott's parallel finding — that warning failures are typically driven by motivated reasoning among decision-makers rather than by an absence of accurate information reaching them — applies directly to the present case.49 The CSIS depletion data is not a secret; it is published, detailed, and repeatedly updated. The failure is not missing information — it is that the institutions positioned to act on it are least able, for the structural reasons above, to draw the conclusion it supports.

If the argument about organized denial is correct, it should produce identifiable institutional patterns. Depletion analyses will be translated into procurement remedies rather than doctrine change, even when the evidence points to a mismatch procurement alone cannot resolve, because that framing preserves organizational legitimacy and budgetary continuity. Crisis response will repeatedly strengthen incumbent contractors and existing procurement channels — not from conspiracy, but because those channels are the default route through which the system processes recognized failure, converting each new piece of evidence — including the evidence this paper presents — into a justification for more procurement rather than less overextension. This prediction is falsifiable: if the post-Epic-Fury policy response generates serious reconsideration of simultaneous-theater doctrine rather than expanded TLAM production lines, the argument of Section VI will require revision. The available evidence, including the FY2027 budget request and the CSIS commentary analyzed above, does not suggest that revision is underway. It is worth being explicit, in the terms of this paper's own evidence taxonomy, about what kind of claim this is. That depletion data exists and is public is Confirmed. That policy responses have so far taken the form of procurement and appropriations requests rather than doctrine review is Reported, documented in the FY2027 budget request and the Baskaran commentary above. That this pattern reflects incentive-driven institutional suppression, rather than good-faith analytical limits within a genuinely difficult problem, is Analyst Inference: the paper does not have, and does not claim to have, direct evidence — internal memos, interviews, or documented instances of a specific analysis being altered or shelved — that any individual or office suppressed a conclusion it privately held. The argument rests on the fit between observed institutional behavior and the antecedent conditions Janis, Allison, and Jones and Silberzahn identify, and on the falsifiable prediction stated above, not on documented intent.

The climate change denial literature offers a further refinement, because it distinguishes types of denial that the phrase "organized denial" otherwise risks flattening into one. Sociological work on climate denial separates epistemic denial (rejecting that the underlying facts are settled), action denial (accepting the facts but rejecting the proposed response), and attributional denial (accepting the facts but rejecting the proposed cause).25 Washington's posture toward the depletion data is overwhelmingly action and attributional denial rather than epistemic denial: the CSIS numbers are not disputed. What is resisted is the attribution — that the numbers are downstream of deindustrialization and defense-sector financialization rather than of insufficient appropriations — and the action that attribution would imply, which is structural reform rather than larger procurement budgets within the existing model. More importantly, the climate literature establishes that organized denial of this kind is rarely a pure cognitive failure. Oreskes and Conway's account of the "doubt is our product" strategy shows that climate denial was actively manufactured by actors with a material stake in delaying the policy response it would have required.26 The same structure of interest operates here. The defense-industrial base identified in Section V has a direct financial stake in a framing that treats depletion as a procurement gap rather than as evidence that its production model is incompatible with the doctrine it is funded to support; admitting the latter would mean admitting the model requires restructuring, not larger contracts. The national-security bureaucracy has an institutional stake in a framing that preserves the doctrine of primacy, because the doctrine is the basis of its budgetary claim and its organizational mission. Organized denial, in this fuller sense, is not only cognitive. It is structural and interested — a policy imaginary that several powerful actors have a material reason to maintain, whether or not any individual within it is acting in bad faith.

A specimen of this pattern appeared in real time as this paper was being finalized, and it is worth naming because of who produced it. In a June 2026 commentary, CSIS's Critical Minerals Security Program director Gracelin Baskaran documented the same dynamic this paper has been describing — Chinese state-linked firms systematically outbidding Western buyers for overseas mineral assets, exemplified by Shenghe Resources' 2025 acquisition of the Ngualla rare-earth deposit in Tanzania over a U.S.-backed counteroffer — and concluded that the United States needs better visibility (a "centralized critical minerals asset registry"), better coordination (a "formal allied early action mechanism"), stronger embassy-level monitoring, and a "rapid-response financing capability" to compete for acquisitions.38 Every one of these recommendations is a Tier One measure in the vocabulary of Section VII below: it treats the problem as one of information and coordination speed within the existing competitive structure. None of it asks why Chinese state-linked capital can move with "speed, certainty, and scale" that fragmented Western financing structurally cannot, or why Chinese firms can pay, as in the Tanzania case, nearly four times the pre-announcement value of an asset and still expect a return — an arithmetic that only works if the asset's value lies in securing a supply chain for a state-coordinated industrial strategy rather than in the asset's standalone return on capital. A registry tells Washington what is for sale ten minutes earlier. It does not change who can credibly bid on it, because the gap is not informational but structural: one side has patient, state-directed capital attached to an industrial strategy, and the other has financing that "can take months to assemble" because it answers to shareholders rather than to a strategy. This is the "window of vulnerability" pattern again — named with precision, and then immediately reframed as a problem this paper's own analytical framework predicts the proposed fix cannot solve.

VII. Minimum Corrective Measures: What the Contradiction Requires

What follows is not a reform agenda for the system this paper has been describing. The system does not produce these outcomes by accident and cannot be repaired by better management of the same incentives. Iran's strategic achievement in this war was not a theatrical battlefield triumph. It was survival, endurance, and the exposure of the costs required for the United States to sustain escalation. The measures below proceed from that diagnosis, not from the assumption that the existing order is coherent and improvable at the margins — and they are organized into two tiers precisely because conflating them is the move that allows the contradiction to go unaddressed.

The first tier consists of measures that address the immediate depletion crisis documented in Sections I through IV: stockpiles, production timelines, and the tungsten supply chain specifically. These measures are necessary. They are also, on their own, a continuation of the framing this paper has been arguing against — they treat the crisis as a procurement and budgeting problem solvable within the existing doctrine of full-spectrum primacy. Presented alone, as they typically are in the policy literature this paper engages with, they function as a way of avoiding the second tier.

Tier One: Containment of the Immediate Depletion Crisis

IMMEDIATE
Establish a Munitions Continuity Fund
Congress should establish a dedicated, multi-year appropriation for Tomahawk, THAAD, and Patriot replenishment that is insulated from the annual appropriations cycle. The Trump administration's proposed $70 billion FY2027 munitions request — a near-threefold increase — is a necessary start, but production rate acceleration requires long-lead-time contracting that cannot function under continuing resolutions. CSIS notes that even after funding approval, some missiles require more than three years between contract and delivery.
NEAR-TERM
Treat Tungsten as a Strategic Reserve Mineral
The National Defense Stockpile currently holds inadequate tungsten reserves relative to sustained high-intensity operations. USGS and DoD should jointly update stockpile targets using Epic Fury burn-rate data as the baseline scenario. The 70% collapse in Chinese APT export volumes since early 2025 means that market-based procurement can no longer be relied upon even in peacetime.
MEDIUM-TERM
Fund the Non-Chinese Supply Chain
South Korea's Sangdong mine (Almonty Industries) and Portugal's Panasqueira mine represent the most immediately scalable non-Chinese tungsten sources; the U.S. military has already issued solicitations for expanded critical mineral production. Both require guaranteed offtake agreements to justify production acceleration. DPA Title III authority should be used aggressively. Oppenheimer's April 2026 price target increase for Almonty signals commercial recognition of this dynamic, but commercial recognition does not substitute for state-backed demand guarantees at the scale the supply gap requires.
STRUCTURAL
Resolve the Containment Contradiction
U.S. trade and sanctions policy toward China should explicitly model the secondary effects on critical mineral supply chains for defense production. Tariff regimes that reduce Chinese industrial scale in sectors that cross-subsidize military-relevant materials processing are not strategically neutral. They accelerate the Time Compression Trap. China's differentiated treatment of gallium/germanium (partially suspended) versus tungsten (controls intact) demonstrates that Beijing is already making these calculations. Washington should make them too.
ANALYTIC
Correct the Epistemic Architecture
IC and DoD should commission an after-action review specifically focused on the gap between pre-war assessments of Iranian missile force resilience and the operational reality. The Winchester Trap was partly a failure of material supply. It was also partly a failure of intelligence assessment — one compounded by the prior Red Sea Houthi campaign's drain on interceptor stocks that was apparently not fully integrated into Epic Fury planning assumptions.

Tier Two: The Choice Tier One Cannot Substitute For

None of the measures above resolve the question raised in Section V: what role is available to a state with America's remaining advantages in an order that is becoming geoeconomic and multipolar rather than security-organized and bipolar. That question cannot be answered by stockpiling tungsten — only by a strategic choice the policy literature this paper engages with does not pose, because posing it means admitting that primacy itself, not merely the means of sustaining it, is the variable in question. The real question is therefore not whether the United States should reconsider grand strategy, but how much material depletion, fiscal strain, and strategic incoherence it is willing to absorb to avoid revising it. The costs of refusal are already being paid: in replenishment timelines the industrial base cannot match, in the WF6 supply rupture that converted a tungsten export control into an unmodeled chip-manufacturing crisis, in radar-less fighters accepted as a "calculated risk," in an allied Pacific deterrent degraded before a shot was fired in that theater. Refusal to revise grand strategy is not a neutral act of preservation. It externalizes costs — onto procurement systems, onto taxpayers who fund replenishment without being told what is being replenished, onto security partners who discover their own deterrents degraded by a campaign fought elsewhere.67

Moments of visible structural crisis are not only diagnostic; they are political openings — the rare occasions when the gap between doctrine and material reality becomes legible outside the policy imaginary, and alternative framings become speakable. In Gramscian terms, this is an organic crisis: a moment when the ruling bloc's contradictions become simultaneously a liability and an opening, and the established order loses its capacity to present itself as self-evident common sense.72 Whether that opening is seized or foreclosed depends on whether organized political actors name what the evidence shows — or whether the organizations that benefit from the existing framing succeed, as before, in converting the crisis into an argument for more of the same.

A clear-eyed account of why Tier Two is politically difficult requires naming its opponents specifically. The five prime contractors — Lockheed Martin, Raytheon/RTX, Northrop Grumman, Boeing, and General Dynamics — benefit from simultaneous-theater primacy because a commitment structure that permanently justifies maximum procurement from the minimum number of suppliers is the optimal demand environment for an oligopoly. The national-security bureaucracy benefits because the doctrine is the organizational basis of its budgetary claims and its revolving-door ecosystem. The think-tank and consultancy complex that bridges these worlds — CSIS, RAND, the Atlantic Council, Booz Allen, SAIC, Leidos — benefits from the procurement-gap framing because it generates demand for analysis that does not threaten the underlying system. Against this coalition, the interests that would benefit from Tier Two revision are diffuse and largely unaware of the stakes: taxpayers, service personnel, allied governments whose deterrents have been silently degraded, and Global South states whose alignment Washington courts while refusing to offer compelling terms. The cross-cutting alliance Tier Two change would require — fiscal conservatives, operational commanders who have seen the depletion data, allied governments willing to say publicly what they say privately, and civil-society actors capable of making this political economy legible to a mass audience — is precisely what the organized denial apparatus of Section VI is designed to prevent from forming. This is not pessimism. It is the political diagnosis Tier Two requires before its implementation can be planned seriously.74

DOCTRINAL
Selective Retrenchment from Simultaneous-Theater Doctrine
The doctrine that requires readiness for simultaneous confrontation with China, Russia, Iran, and aligned regional formations is the demand side of the depletion equation. No supply-side fix changes the arithmetic if the demand side keeps expanding. A revised posture — closer to offshore balancing or selective engagement focused on a smaller set of core commitments — is not a concession to weakness. It is the only adjustment that brings doctrine into alignment with the industrial base this paper has described, rather than asking the industrial base to someday catch up to the doctrine.
INDUSTRIAL
Industrial Policy Realism: Accepting Permanently Higher Costs
Guaranteed offtake agreements and DPA Title III authority, used in isolation, ask non-Chinese tungsten producers to compete against an integrated civilian-military production system without the civilian-scale demand that makes such systems viable. The honest alternative is to accept, explicitly and durably, that strategic-sector production will run at a structural cost premium indefinitely — not as a transitional subsidy until competitiveness is achieved, because for chokepoint minerals it likely will not be — and to build that premium into defense budgeting as a permanent feature rather than a temporary correction.
GEOECONOMIC
Engagement with Middle Powers on Their Terms
India, Brazil, the Gulf states, Turkey, Indonesia, and Vietnam are not available as Cold War-style aligned partners, and treating critical-minerals diplomacy as an extension of bloc competition will read, to these states, as an offer to choose sides they have no incentive to choose. Engagement that succeeds in a geoeconomic order will look like the arrangements these states already build with each other and with China: commodity-for-investment deals, processing joint ventures, infrastructure financing without security conditionality. The alternative — continuing to frame mineral diplomacy as alignment — will most likely produce polite non-alignment and continued dependence.
REFLEXIVE
Rethinking Sanctions and Export-Control Blowback
Every U.S. sanctions and tariff action analyzed in this paper has a secondary effect on the supply chains the U.S. itself depends on. A serious response requires a standing interagency process that models these effects before action, not after — but more fundamentally, it requires acknowledging that the sanctions toolkit has been used as a substitute for industrial strategy for two decades, and that substitution is itself part of what produced the Time Compression Trap. Continued reliance on sanctions and export controls as the primary instrument of economic statecraft, absent the industrial base to absorb the retaliatory consequences, deepens the contradiction this paper describes rather than managing it.

These four items are directional, not operational. "Selective retrenchment" does not specify which theaters would be deprioritized or on what timeline; "engagement with middle powers on their terms" does not specify what the United States would offer New Delhi or Riyadh that it is not already offering. Specifying either would require a force-posture review and a bilateral-negotiation analysis, respectively — each a separate paper's worth of work, resting on classified force-planning assumptions this analysis does not have access to. What this section establishes is the direction any serious correction has to move in and the coalition problem it runs into immediately; it does not establish the implementation plan.

VIII. Conclusion: Reading the Clock

Tungsten is a simple metal. It is heavy, it is hard, and it does not melt. Those properties make it irreplaceable in the weapons that the United States fires at the beginning of every air campaign — the penetrating warheads, the shaped charges, the armor-piercing rounds. It is also, for precisely that reason, the most honest indicator of where American war-making capacity now stands relative to the political economy that is supposed to sustain it.

The APT price above $3,000/mtu is not a commodity story. It is the market form of a structural fact: American war-making now depends on industrial conditions its own political economy helped dismantle. Washington continues to act as if this is a supply-chain management problem. The 15-company whitelist is not a trade policy deviation. It is Beijing occupying, from the standpoint of industrial capacity, a position the United States vacated from the standpoint of financial efficiency. The clock was not set by China. It was set by the decades of elite choice that offshored production, financialized defense, and converted imperial reach into a substitute for industrial competence. And the organized denial that prevents Washington from reading that clock accurately is not a failure of intelligence — it is the political economy of obstruction: the same actors who built the system, who profit from its continuation, and who are paid to produce the analyses that name its symptoms while protecting its causes.

To say that the United States emerged depleted from a 39-day war is still not sufficient. The larger point is that Epic Fury exposed the contradiction between hegemonic doctrine and the material base available to sustain it. A state may remain overwhelmingly destructive in absolute terms and yet prove unable to support the strategic burdens it has assigned itself. Iran's strategic achievement, in that limited but real sense, lay in enduring the campaign while helping force that contradiction into view.

But Iran and China are not the only actors whose behavior the clock should be read against. The states that did not appear in this paper's data — the middle powers hedging across both blocs, building the commodity arrangements, payment systems, and infrastructure financing that constitute a geoeconomic order indifferent to security alignment — are the ones whose adaptation is furthest along. They are not waiting to see whether the United States resolves its munitions depletion. They have already concluded that the resolution, whatever it is, will not determine the terms on which they engage with the world economy. The tungsten clock measures how fast American capacity is being consumed. It does not measure, and cannot measure, how fast the world it was built to police has already moved on.

Washington refuses to admit this. It continues to describe depletion as a procurement lag, overextension as a readiness gap, and industrial decay as an investment shortfall. Those framings are not wrong as descriptions of symptoms. They are organized denial: the policy world protecting its own legitimacy by refusing to name the system producing the symptoms. The problem is not that American decision-makers have failed to notice the warning signs. The problem is that they built a political economy in which noticing the warning signs would require admitting that the whole imperial model is failing.

That refusal is not cost-free. Every year the strategic class avoids revising primacy, costs accumulate where the doctrine does not look: in the WF6 rupture no procurement budget modeled, in the radar-less aircraft accepted as a calculated risk, in the allied Pacific deterrent degraded before the relevant theater was engaged. The refusal to revise grand strategy shifts the burden onto procurement systems, operational readiness, and institutional legitimacy — converting a structural contradiction into an indefinitely deferred reckoning whose costs are paid by systems that cannot say no to the doctrine demanding them.

Epic Fury matters not because it proved that the United States is weak, but because it showed how a state can remain tactically destructive while becoming strategically difficult to sustain. The campaign made visible the gap between doctrine and reproduction: the United States could still strike hard, but it could not do so without consuming inventories, tightening allied margins, and exposing production timelines measured in years rather than weeks. That is the meaning of the clock. It does not measure a sudden collapse. It measures the accumulated distance between a doctrine of primacy and the material base required to keep that doctrine credible — a distance that China's export-control architecture did not create, but made legible, usable as leverage, and structurally difficult to close on any timeline the doctrine itself assumes.
